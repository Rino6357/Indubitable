Jorrin hated carrying grain. It wasn’t because it was heavy, no. In fact, he could carry more if it weren’t for his lack of eight hands.

Jorrin hated carrying grain because he would have to listen to Odo’s ramblings about honest work afterward.

The door of the old brewery slammed open as he threw the final sack into the bin with “Drop offs” scribbled onto it. The smell of yeast and oak drifted through the room. Sunlight filtered between roots thicker than logs and painted stripes across the floor.

The old brewer was a stout but tall man, his bulbous nose just above a scraggly beard that desperately needed some pulling. He fidgeted with the wooden knob as he kept the door open, as always. Maybe he should have been a carpenter.

“Now, why do you always go and ruin perfectly good labor?” Odo grumbled, twirling his mustache with his other hand.

Jorrin turned his head and smirked.

“I’m sorry, uncle, I’ll make sure to sweat a little harder and grunt a little louder on the next sack of gra–”

“Now, listen, you big brute!” Odo bellowed, jabbing a finger into Jorrin’s chest. “You’re obviously too strong for any labor to get through to you, so maybe I’ll just have you read a book to teach you a thing or two about respect!”

Jorrin gasped playfully and smiled.

“A book? Oh, no! Anything but that!”

Odo narrowed his eyes. He grabbed at Jorrin’s collar and shook. Jorrin remained where he was. Odo frowned and shook harder, his feet sliding against the dirt, Jorrin still as the roots above.

“Why are you up so early anyway, uncle?” Jorrin asked.

“Mary is coming over to visit in the afternoon. This place is still a mess after last night… Those cityfolk never fail to make a mess, but at least they spend a pretty penny.”

Jorrin’s smile faded.

“Damn cityfolk.”

“Anyway, I need you to go to Lukin’s to grab some things. I wanted to put together something special for her today.”

Odo handed him a small piece of paper.

“Ok, I’ll be off, then.”

Jorrin pocketed the note.

He couldn’t help but think of his uncle’s “something special” as he turned toward the bar. Odo loved to make things out of seemingly worthless stuff.

Jorrin ran his palm across the edge of the wooden platform. It was smooth. Odo had dedicated a lot of time into it.

Behind the bar was a shelf, also carved into the wall, stacked with bottles of every shape and size. There were cylindrical bottles, spherical bottles, tiny bottles, and gigantic bottles.

There were even star shaped bottles.

His uncle was indeed a man of many skills.

Walking around the platform, he entered behind the bar. He swung open the cupboard labeled “Odo” where the box of coins for everyday use usually sat, but the shelf was empty.

“Uncle, I can’t find the coin box. Did you move it?”

“I hid it upstairs in your room yesterday. I didn’t want to take any risks with those… people.”

It was common for folk from the upper terraces to act roughly with them. Jorrin tried not to dwell on it.

Raising himself from behind the bar, he was able to take in the entire room.

Huge roots emerged from the far walls, accompanied by enormous growth rings. It was the shape of a half circle, filled with tables and chairs everywhere. Those were empty now, but patrons would struggle to find a place to sit after sunset.

The front door lay next to the drop off bin, with square windows on each side. The panes let in the morning light to catch floating dust motes.

Behind him was a gate toward another room in the shape of another half circle. It smelled of fermenting grain.

Inside were dozens of barrels, a kitchen in the center, and stairs on each side.

One led downward to a storage cellar.

The other led upward to his room.
