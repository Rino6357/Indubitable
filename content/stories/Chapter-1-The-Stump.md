Chapter 1: The Stump

Jorrin hated carrying grain. It wasn’t because it was heavy, no. In fact, he could carry more if it weren’t for his lack of eight hands. Jorrin hated carrying grain because he would have to listen to Odo’s ramblings about honest work afterward. The door of the old brewery slammed open as he threw the final sack into the bin with “Drop offs” scribbled onto it. The smell of yeast and oak drifted through the room. Sunlight filtered between roots thicker than logs and painted stripes across the floor.
The old brewer was a stout but tall man, his bulbous nose just above a scraggly beard that desperately needed some pulling. He fidgeted with the wooden knob as he kept the door open, as always. Maybe he should have been a carpenter.
“Now, why do you always go and ruin perfectly good labor?” Odo grumbled, twirling his mustache with his other hand.
Jorrin turned his head and smirked. “I’m sorry, uncle, I’ll make sure to sweat a little harder and grunt a little louder on the next sack of gra–”
“Now, listen, you big brute!” Odo bellowed, jabbing a finger into Jorrin’s chest. “You’re obviously too strong for any labor to get through to you, so maybe I’ll just have you read a book to teach you a thing or two about respect!”
Jorrin gasped playfully and smiled. “A book? Oh, no! Anything but that!” 
Odo narrowed his eyes. He grabbed at Jorrin’s collar and shook. Jorrin remained where he was. Odo frowned and shook harder, his feet sliding against the dirt, Jorrin still as the roots above.
“Why are you up so early anyway, uncle?” Jorrin asked.
“Mary is coming over to visit in the afternoon. This place is still a mess after last night… Those cityfolk never fail to make a mess, but at least they spend a pretty penny.” Odo grumbled. Jorrin’s smile faded and he looked at his uncle in concern.
“Damn cityfolk.” He whispered.
“Anyway, I need you to go to Lukin’s to grab some things. I wanted to put together something special for her today.” Odo said, handing Jorrin a small piece of paper. Jorrin stared at him for a second. 
“Ok, I’ll be off, then.” Jorrin smiled, pocketing the piece of paper. 
He couldn’t help but think of his uncle’s “something special” as he turned toward the bar. Odo loved to make things out of seemingly worthless stuff. Jorrin ran his palm across the edge of the wooden platform. It was smooth. Odo had dedicated a lot of time into it. Behind the bar was a shelf, also carved into the wall, stacked with bottles of every shape and size. There were cylindrical bottles, spherical bottles, tiny bottles, and gigantic bottles. There were even star shaped bottles! His uncle was indeed a man of many skills. Walking around the platform, he entered behind the bar. He swung open the cupboard labeled “Odo” where the box of coins for everyday use usually sat, but the shelf was empty.
“Uncle, I can’t find the coin box. Did you move it?”
“I hid it upstairs in your room yesterday. I didn’t want to take any risks with those… people.” He said wearily. It was common for the folk from the upper terraces to act roughly with them. Jorrin tried not to dwell on it.
Raising himself from behind the bar, he was able to take in the entire room. Huge roots emerged from the far walls, accompanied by enormous growth rings. It was the shape of a half circle, filled with tables and chairs everywhere. Those were empty now, but patrons would struggle to find a place to sit after sunset. The front door lay next to the drop off bin, with square windows on each side. The panes let in the morning light to catch floating dust motes. Behind him was a gate toward another room in the shape of another half circle. It smelled of fermenting grain. Inside were dozens of barrels, a kitchen in the center, and stairs on each side. One led downward to a storage cellar, and the other led upward to his room.
His room wasn’t anything special, at least not for those who did not know where to look. The bed faced the door—“so you don’t forget to leave it,” Odo had once said. The desk still had knife marks where Jorrin had misjudged a carving. The closet still missed one of its doors from when he accidentally pulled on it too hard in a rush. Jorrin crouched and opened the drawer under his desk. He pulled harder and removed the drawer entirely, revealing a shelf of books. “Teoskopian Tales,” “Plants: a History,” “Ancient Oceans,” and a plethora of other books. They were all there. Good. Odo hadn’t found them. He returned the drawer and turned to leave his room. Oh, the coins. Opening the drawer once more, he took the box of coins and went back down the stairs.
“Found it?” Odo asked. Jorrin answered with a nod and left. Closing the door behind him, he saw the big sign above the front door that read “The Stump” in bright red letters. It was fitting. Odo once told him that it used to be a giant tree as old as time itself. It wasn’t hard to believe, considering the size. The roots extended so far that he and his uncle had frequent land disputes with their neighbors over them. Well, it was home.
Turning, he saw the steep fall to the lower terraces. Crops were most of what he could see at this level. Pretty much only farmers lived this low. Low enough to see the walls at the foot of the mountain. 
Jorrin grabbed one of the sticks leaning against the stump and began his way up. Mist and clouds covered most of what he could see when he looked up. Armadon was a large mountain. Without the terraces carved into it, getting anywhere safely would be impossible for its people. Using his stick to keep stable, he climbed up the mountain step by step. The closest shopping district was several hundred terraces above The Stump, but Jorrin stopped minding a long time ago. The stairs wound between farms and orchards, each terrace held up by cobblestone walls older than even Odo could remember. Some were little more than strips of dirt supporting a single family. Others stretched wide enough to hold dozens of fields. 
Farmers were already at work. Some raised their hands in greeting as he passed.
“Morning, jorrin!” Plint Harrow was now getting so old his joints would crack as he plowed his fields.
“Morning, mister Harrow.” Jorrin waved back.
“Tell Odo he still owes me a drink!” Old age had not yet robbed him of his fire, Jorrin thought.
“He says you still owe him three!” He responded. The old farmer barked a laugh.
Jorrin continued his climb, moving from one terrace to the next. Sometimes he’d use the stick to hop over some steps, just to add some excitement to the climb. He was about half way to Lukin’s when a loud burst of laughter drifted down above him.
Three men were making their way down the terraces. Their mount, a massive cliff-ibex with curling horns larger than wagon wheels, picked its path carefully between the terraces while its riders swayed in the saddle. 
On closer look, the men wore coats. Their sleeves were lined with silver thread and their polished boots reflected the morning light. Cityfolk. Jorrin never understood the appeal in clothes too clean to work in.
One of the cityfolk nearly fell from the saddle on the ibex, catching himself by its horn at the last moment before bursting into laughter. The beast sighed through its nostrils and continued its careful descent. One ought to be more gentle with an Ibex. Poor thing.
Jorrin picked up the smell of ale as the large animal approached him. Drinking this early in the morning? Where were they going, anyway? He wasn’t sure if they knew themselves.
“Oi!” One of them shouted, squinting down at him. “You’re the brewer’s boy, aren’t you?”
Jorrin frowned. Their faces did seem familiar. Couldn’t they at least have remembered his name?
“The brewer!” another slurred. “Best ale on the mountain!”
“The only reason any of us ever come this far down,” the third added.
The first man raised a bottle toward Jorrin. “Tell him we’ll be back next week.” The others cheered at the prospect. The Ibex seemed considerably less excited. 
As they continued down the mountain, their voices faded into the terraces below.

“…telling you, they found another one…”

“What, down there?”

“…your voice down, fool.”

A burst of laughter followed. Jorrin frowned. Found another what?
Jorrin paused at a landing sometime after the Ibex had passed him. He turned and looked over the edge. 
The cityfolk had steered off from The Stump, which had shrunk to a speck among the fields below. He hadn’t expected them to go there anyway. Even they had to know they had had too much to drink. 
Terraces wound around the mountain beneath him like steps for giants. Fields gave away to homes, homes to markets, and markets to districts he had never gone to visit. He couldn’t see it up here, but he knew that far below, the great wall encircled the foot of Armadon. 
Past that wall lay only mist. Jorrin stared at it.
Everyone spoke of the fog as if it were the edge of the world. Odo always said that nothing out there was worth the risk. 
Jorrin gripped his climbing stick and turned back up the mountain.
The terraces grew busier as he climbed. Fields gave way to workshops and homes. Farmers turned into craftsmen and merchants, and the familiar smell of soil was gradually replaced by that of coal and baking bread. The sound of hammering and bells ringing drifted from above him. A large stone wall indicated a massive terrace. 
By the time he stepped foot in the shopping district, the morning bustle had already flooded the streets. 
District was not the right word. The terrace was large enough to be called a village. Maybe even a town. Wooden signs hung above storefronts carved into the mountainside. Children darted between pack animals while workers hauled carts of goods. Hawkers shouted wares from carts among the people, and merchants beckoned customers from stalls lining the road. 
Jorrin stepped aside as a pair of soot faced blacksmith apprentices carried a glowing iron bar through the street. Wasn’t that dangerous?
After some pushing his way through the crowd, Jorrin found Lukin’s shop near the far end of the terrace beneath a crooked wooden sign that read:
LUKIN’S GOODS AND ODDMENTS

Jorrin had never figured out what an oddment was, but Lukin seemed to have plenty of them.
The moment Jorrin stepped inside, the noise of the street vanished behind him. The shop smelled of cedar, lamp oil, and old paper. Shelves stretched from floor to ceiling, crammed with everything from rope and lanterns to jars of dried herbs. Small wooden plaques hung from each shelf in Lukin’s careful handwriting. 
Walking deeper into the shop, one of the shelves held a rusted brass compass. Another had a fragment of blue stone, and another a book written in a language that Jorrin had never seen before. He remembered those from when he was a child. For some reason, they never seemed to leave the shelves.
“Hello, Jorrin.” A voice came from somewhere above. Jorrin looked up to find Lukin halfway up a rolling ladder, balancing a crate on his head. 
“You know,” Lukin said, “most customers use the front of the shop.”
“I did use the front.”
“Oh. Maybe I am becoming senile after all.” Lukin paused, but laughed worriedly. “Well. What are we after today?”
Jorrin unfolded Odo’s note. “Uhh. Sunseed oil. Honey. Dried thyme.” He read.
“Ah.” Lukin sighed. 

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“What?”

“Your uncle is… cooking.” Before Jorrin could retort, Lukin hopped down the ladder and began walking toward the front of the shop. “No time to waste, then.” 
He grabbed a bottle of sunseed oil from a nearby shelf and handed it to Jorrin.
“How’s the reading?”

Jorrin blinked. “The reading?”

“You always look confused when you’re halfway through a book.” 

“I do not!” 

“You do.” Lukin said, climbing another rolling ladder. “Well? What is it this time?”

“Teoskopian Tales.” 

Lukin paused with a jar of honey in his hands.

Jorrin sighed. “I’m not halfway through, though. I can’t seem to keep up with anything it says. I’m kind of stumped, if I’m being honest.”

“That one tends to do that,” Lukin said after a moment, carefully climbing down the ladder. “Most people pretend they understood it anyway.”
He carefully placed the jar of honey in Jorrin’s hands.

“Oh. Then I guess you won’t be able to help me.” Jorrin sighed.

“I never said I didn’t understand it,” he added. “But it is not a book you should be reading while working at your uncle’s brewery.”

“What, why?” Jorrin asked. “It’s just a book.”

Lukin didn’t look at him. “That’s usually how it starts.” He walked to another shelf, ignoring Jorrin’s question. He returned with a jar of dried thyme, tying the lid shut before handing it over. His fingers lingered on the label a moment too long. 

“That’s everything,” he said, putting his palm out toward Jorrin. 

Jorrin slipped a few coins to Lukin and gathered the items into his arms. For a moment, Lukin didn’t move. Then he turned away, climbing back up the ladder.
“Tell your uncle to be careful,” he said, without looking down. 

“Careful with what?” Jorrin asked.

But Lukin only waved a hand vaguely, already lost among the shelves. 

Jorrin frowned and turned away, stepping back into the noise of the terrace. The smell of cedar and oil faded instantly into smoke, iron, and bread crust drifting through the market air. He had left the shop, but Lukin’s words lingered with him.
Walking through the crowds again, he almost dropped the sunseed oil as someone brushed past him. It reminded him to put the items in his backsack.
As he neared the path to the lower terraces, he noticed men in ornate red uniforms walking through the streets, the townspeople steering away from them. One stood before a despondent merchant with his hand outstretched. Another was speaking to a hawker whose smile looked increasingly forced. It seemed a little early for the tribute collections.
The higher terraces blurred behind him as he descended the mountain. The solid sound of boots against stone soon turned to the comforting sound of soft dirt. By the time he reached the lower terraces, the noise of the market had thinned into wind and distant hammering. The atmosphere seemed to settle around him, and the air finally grew heavier with soil.
On his way back to the Stump, a pair of armored wardens passed him on the road. Their helmets bore angled plumes, marking them as officers. Jorrin stepped aside to let them pass. They didn’t spare him a glance. 
The Stump rose from the lower fields like an ancient monument, its roots sprawling through the terraces around it. Smoke curled from its upper vents. The smell of brewing grain drifted into the wind. Home, Jorrin thought.
As he approached the front of the brewery, Lukin’s words returned to him. 

“That’s usually how it starts.” 

Jorrin shook his head. Books couldn’t start anything, people did. 

The front door of The Stump creaked slightly in the wind. 

Jorrin paused.

Odo always kept it shut.
